What a Lifetime of Work Teaches a Storyteller

Long before I thought seriously about writing books, work was teaching me things a writer needs to know.

Most jobs do not look like writing lessons while you are doing them. You are trying to solve the problem in front of you, take care of a customer, find the right part, make the route, meet the deadline or simply get through a difficult day.

Years later, you realize you were studying people the entire time.

Listen to what people mean

People rarely describe a problem in perfect language. They tell you what they saw, heard or felt. Sometimes they start with the wrong conclusion. Your job is to listen past the first explanation and find the useful detail.

Characters work the same way. People do not always say what they mean, especially when pride, fear, love or embarrassment is involved. Good dialogue has something underneath it.

Details solve problems

In practical work, a tiny detail can separate the right answer from an expensive mistake. Storytelling rewards the same attention. A generic room is forgettable. One familiar object can make the room belong to somebody.

The detail should not be there just to prove the writer noticed it. It should tell us something.

Everybody has expertise

A title does not tell you how much somebody knows. I have met people whose formal education was limited but whose understanding of machines, customers, roads, weather or human nature was remarkable.

Fiction becomes more believable when it respects the intelligence people develop through living and working.

Problems rarely arrive one at a time

Real working days have interruptions. The phone rings while something else is already wrong. A simple job reveals another problem. Somebody needs an answer before you have one.

That pressure is useful to fiction. Characters become visible when plans stop working.

People are the part you remember

After enough years, individual workdays blur together. What remains are people: the funny customer, the difficult boss, the person who taught you something, the one who believed in you, the coworker whose saying you can still hear.

That may be the biggest lesson work gave me as a storyteller. Jobs create the setting. People create the story.

That idea runs through Three-Quarters Green and much of what I write.
