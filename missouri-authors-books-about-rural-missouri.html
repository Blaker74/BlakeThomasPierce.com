Missouri Authors and Books About Rural Missouri

A starting place for readers who want stories that sound like home, or want to understand a place they never lived.

Missouri sits in the middle of the map and somehow manages to be several places at once. There are river towns, the hills and hollers of the Ozarks, big cities on both ends of the state and, down in the southeast corner, the flat Bootheel farm country where I come from. Each of those places has produced writing worth reading.

This is not a ranked list or a complete history. It is a short, honest guide for readers looking for Missouri authors, books set in rural Missouri and stories about the kind of working people who live there.

The river: Mark Twain’s Missouri

Any conversation about Missouri writers starts with Samuel Clemens, who grew up in Hannibal on the Mississippi River and wrote as Mark Twain. The fictional town of St. Petersburg in The Adventures of Tom Sawyer is modeled on Hannibal, and Life on the Mississippi draws on his years on the river. Under the humor, Twain paid close attention to how ordinary people talked, worked and got by, which is a big part of why those books still hold up.

The farm: Laura Ingalls Wilder at Rocky Ridge

Many readers are surprised to learn that Laura Ingalls Wilder wrote her Little House books while living at Rocky Ridge Farm near Mansfield, Missouri. The books are set in other states, but they were written by a Missouri farm woman looking back on a childhood of making do, and that backward look at hard, ordinary years is something a lot of rural writers recognize.

The hills: the Ozarks in fiction

Daniel Woodrell’s Winter’s Bone is set in the Missouri Ozarks and follows a teenage girl holding her family together in hard circumstances. It is not a gentle book, but it takes rural poverty and family loyalty seriously, without looking down on the people in it. Readers who want rural Missouri fiction with an edge often start there.

Missouri-born voices

Missouri also gave American literature writers who made their names elsewhere. Langston Hughes was born in Joplin, and Maya Angelou was born in St. Louis. Their work is not about rural Missouri, but it belongs on any list of writers the state can claim.

The Bootheel and cotton country

Southeast Missouri does not show up on bookshelves nearly as often as the river or the Ozarks. It is Delta country: flat farmland, cotton and rice fields, small towns, the Mississippi close by and families who have worked that ground for generations. Readers who want a feel for that kind of life can look just across the state line to John Grisham’s A Painted House, set on a cotton farm in rural Arkansas in the 1950s. It is about a boy, a family getting by on a cotton farm and a farmhouse that has never been painted. Readers of Three-Quarters Green, with its half-finished green trailer, will notice the echo.

Where my own book fits: Three-Quarters Green is my novel about Lake Farmer, a boy who grows up in a twelve-by-sixty mobile home painted three-quarters green. It draws on the texture of a working-class Southeast Missouri childhood: trailers, old cars, early jobs, family sayings and people solving problems with whatever they have. I wrote it from inside that world, not looking at it from the outside.

What to look for in a good rural Missouri book

  • Work that is real. Farm work, factory work, truck work, shift work. In a good rural story, jobs shape people instead of just filling a line about what a character does.
  • Talk that sounds right. Missouri speech changes from one corner of the state to the other. A writer who listens closely gets it right without turning it into a joke.
  • Dignity without polish. The best books neither romanticize hard times nor sneer at them. They let people be funny, stubborn, proud, tired and loving all at once.
  • Place as more than scenery. The river, the hills or the flat fields should matter to what happens, the way weather and distance matter in real life.

If those are the kinds of stories you look for, you might also like my guides to books about growing up poor and working-class America and rural American fiction about family and memory, or my essay on growing up in a trailer in rural Missouri.
