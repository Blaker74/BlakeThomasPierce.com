How Ordinary Memories Become Stories

Big events announce themselves. Ordinary memories do not. They sit quietly for years until one detail brings an entire room, person or season of life back at once.

People sometimes think a life has to be extraordinary before it is worth writing about. I think almost the opposite is true. The things that connect most deeply are often the things nobody thought to photograph.

The sound of a screen door. A particular car in the driveway. The way somebody pronounced a word. A stove that heated one room better than another. A road you traveled so many times you stopped seeing it. Those details may seem too small to matter while they are happening. Years later, they can hold an entire period of your life.

Memory keeps strange records

We do not remember our lives like a calendar. We remember fragments. Sometimes a major event becomes blurry while an insignificant object stays perfectly clear.

That is useful to a storyteller. The object can become the doorway. Instead of beginning with a statement about childhood, begin with the thing that childhood looked, sounded or smelled like. The reader can walk through that doorway with you.

Specific details become universal

Writers can be afraid that a detail is too personal for other people to understand. Often the opposite happens. The more specific a memory feels, the more likely it is to remind a reader of something different but emotionally similar from his or her own life.

Your reader may never have lived in your house, ridden in your car or known your family. But the reader probably knows what it feels like to miss a place that no longer exists the way it once did.

Objects can carry people

An old watch is not only a watch when you remember who handed it to you. A beat-up vehicle is not only transportation when it carried your family through a dangerous night. A cheap set of steps can become part of the story of a first home.

The writer's job is not to insist that an object is meaningful. It is to show enough of the life around it that the reader understands why it became meaningful.

Do not polish the life out of it

Memory is uneven. Families are uneven. Good stories do not need to pretend everything was wonderful or everything was terrible. Humor can sit beside grief. Embarrassment can sit beside love. Hardship can exist beside gratitude.

That mixture is part of what gives a remembered life its shape.

Why I keep returning to ordinary things

Three-Quarters Green grew from the belief that the ordinary pieces deserve attention. A life does not become meaningful only when something dramatic happens. Meaning accumulates quietly.

Sometimes you do not know which moment mattered until decades later.

That is why I write them down.
