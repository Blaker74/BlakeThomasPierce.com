Grief, Memory and the People We Carry

Why the small details hurt the most, and why they are worth writing down anyway.

Grief has a way of rearranging memory. Things you never thought twice about, like the sound of somebody’s truck pulling in, a phrase they always used or the way they took their coffee, suddenly become the most valuable things you own. The big events stay with you, but it is the ordinary details that ambush you in the grocery store years later.

I write songs and stories, and some of both carry grief. That is not because I set out to write sad things. It is because when you write honestly about family and working people, loss is already in the room.

Grief makes ordinary things valuable

While somebody is alive, their habits can be the most normal thing in the world. Sometimes they are even annoying. After they are gone, those same habits turn into evidence that they were here. That shift, from ordinary to precious, is one of the main ideas running through Three-Quarters Green: the smallest memories can become the ones we would give anything to experience once more.

Writers sometimes go looking for dramatic material. Grief teaches you that the drama is usually in the small stuff. A chair nobody sits in. A handwriting you recognize on an old envelope. A recipe nobody else makes quite right.

Working-class families grieve while they keep working

In a lot of working families, grief does not get much room. The bills still come. Somebody still has to go to work Monday. Kids still need supper. People grieve in the truck on the way to a job site, or late at night after everybody else is asleep, or by fixing something the person they lost used to fix.

That kind of grief is easy to miss in stories, because it is quiet and practical. But it is real, and I think it deserves a place on the page. When fiction shows people carrying loss while still showing up for each other, readers who have lived that way recognize themselves.

Memory is not a recording

Anyone who has compared notes with a brother, sister or cousin knows that two people can remember the same day completely differently. Grief makes that even more true. We polish some memories and lose others. We remember how something felt more clearly than what actually happened.

That is part of what fiction is good for. Three-Quarters Green is a novel, not a memoir, and fiction gave me a way to take the texture of a working-class childhood and build a story around the larger truth inside it, without pretending to be a perfect record. I wrote more about that in From Memory to Manuscript.

If you want to write about someone you lost

I am not a counselor, and writing is not a substitute for the people and help we need when grief gets heavy. But if you have been thinking about putting a memory down on paper, here are a few places to start:

  • Start with one object. A coat, a tool, a coffee cup. Describe it plainly and let the memories come to it instead of trying to tell the whole life at once.
  • Write down how they talked. Sayings, jokes, the words they mispronounced on purpose. Voices fade faster than faces, and they are a gift to the rest of the family.
  • Leave the hard parts in. People are not saints after they die. Honest memories, with the stubbornness and the bad days, usually feel more like the real person.
  • You do not have to share it. Some writing is for a book. Some is for a drawer. Both count.

What writing can do

Writing does not bring anybody back. What it can do is keep a person’s shape in the world a little longer, so that grandkids who never met them can know how they laughed, or what they did when the money ran out, or what they said on the porch on summer nights.

That is a big part of why I write about family, memory and working people. The older I get, the more I understand how much of a life lives in memory, and how easily it disappears if nobody writes it down.
