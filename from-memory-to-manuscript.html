From Memory to Manuscript: Turning Real Life Into Fiction

Real life can give a writer material. It does not automatically give the writer a story.

Memory arrives in pieces. A room. A voice. The smell of an old vehicle. A sentence somebody said once and never knew you would remember. Fiction asks us to take those pieces and build something that can stand on its own.

Start with the detail that refuses to leave

The most useful memory is not always the biggest event. Often it is the detail that stayed behind after everything else blurred. That detail can become the doorway into a scene because it carries emotion without needing to explain the emotion first.

Instead of beginning with a summary of an entire childhood, a writer can begin with the object, sound or place that makes the childhood visible.

Choose instead of transcribing

Life contains too much repetition for a novel. Real conversations wander. Real days include long stretches in which nothing advances. A manuscript needs selection.

The writer decides which moments reveal character, create tension or change what happens next. That is not dishonesty. It is storytelling.

Emotional truth and literal transcription are not the same thing. Fiction can rearrange events while still respecting what an experience meant.

Let characters become characters

A person inspired by somebody real eventually has to function inside the story. The fictional character needs motives, contradictions and choices that serve the book rather than a private record of history.

That distance can also be an act of respect. Fiction should not require turning every private detail of another person's life into public property.

Use sensory memory carefully

Specific details make a scene believable, but more detail is not automatically better. The right two details can do more work than a paragraph of inventory.

Ask what the viewpoint character would notice. A mechanic may notice a sound. A child may notice the frightening space beneath a house. Somebody grieving may notice an ordinary object because the person who used it is gone.

Do not polish away the voice

Revision should make a story clearer, stronger and more deliberate. It should not necessarily make every person sound alike.

Regional language, work language and family language can carry history. The goal is not to exaggerate speech into a costume. The goal is to preserve enough of its rhythm that the people on the page feel as though they came from somewhere.

Then let the story leave home

At some point, a story inspired by memory has to become more than memory. Readers bring their own fathers, mothers, jobs, childhood homes, losses and victories into the book.

That is when something personal can become something shared.

Much of the creative ground behind Three-Quarters Green lives in that territory: ordinary details, work, family and the strange way years can change the meaning of a moment.

The memory may be where a story begins.

The manuscript is where it learns to belong to the reader too.
