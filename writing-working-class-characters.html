Writing Working-Class Characters Without Turning Them Into Stereotypes

A believable character is more than a job title, an accent, a paycheck or a hardship. People who work with their hands deserve the same complexity on the page as anyone else.

Working-class life is often written from the outside. When that happens, characters can become symbols instead of people. The mechanic is only a mechanic. The factory worker is only tired. The poor family exists only to be pitied. Real life is messier and much more interesting.

A person can come home exhausted and still laugh until his stomach hurts. Somebody can struggle financially and refuse to think of himself as a victim. A man who never learned to read well may understand machines, people and survival better than somebody with three degrees. None of those things cancel the others out.

Start with what the person notices

One of the quickest ways to make a character feel real is to pay attention to what that person would notice first. A mechanic may hear a bad bearing before anyone else knows something is wrong. A salesman remembers the customer's truck, the route to the shop and the problem from six months ago. Somebody raised without much money may notice waste that another character never sees.

Those details tell the reader who a person is without stopping the story to explain him.

Work changes language

Every kind of work develops its own vocabulary. People shorten phrases, invent nicknames and use words that make perfect sense inside their world. That language can give fiction texture, but it should serve the character rather than become a costume.

The goal is not to spell every word phonetically or exaggerate a rural voice until the reader has to decode it. A few natural turns of phrase can do more than a page of forced dialect.

Dignity matters more than polish

A character does not need to speak perfectly, live beautifully or make every good decision to have dignity. Dignity comes from letting the reader see the whole person: what he fears, what he protects, what makes him proud, what embarrasses him and what he would do for somebody he loves.

The people who look ordinary from a distance are usually anything but ordinary when you stand close enough to hear their stories.

Let people contradict themselves

Real people are inconsistent. A stern father can be tender in a moment nobody expects. A quiet person can become hilarious around family. Somebody can hate change while secretly dreaming about a different life. Contradictions are not writing mistakes when they come from character. They are often where character begins.

Do not confuse hardship with identity

Poverty, grief and difficult work can shape a life, but they should not become the only thing a character is allowed to be. People living through hard circumstances still fall in love, tease each other, get jealous, make ridiculous purchases, dream too big, waste time and remember things that nobody else would consider important.

That is one of the ideas underneath Three-Quarters Green. The surroundings matter, but the people matter more.

The test I keep coming back to

If a character were sitting across the table from me, would I recognize him as a person—or only recognize what category I put him in?

If the category is louder than the person, there is more work to do.
