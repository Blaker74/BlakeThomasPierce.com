The Story Behind the Story

Growing Up in a Trailer in Rural Missouri

Before it became material for a novel, it was simply home.

Some childhood memories announce themselves as important. Others do not. They are just ordinary details: the heat of a Missouri summer, the sound of an old trailer, a stove warming a room in winter, or somebody finding a way to make do with what was available.

I grew up in a 12-by-60 trailer. We did not have air conditioning. In winter, we heated the place with a potbelly stove, and one of the things that helped keep that stove going was free pine wood scraps from Heartland Wood Products.

At the time, none of that felt like material for a book. It was life.

When making do was normal

Children usually do not measure their homes against the world the way adults do. Home is simply the place where your family is. You notice whether you are hot or cold, whether supper smells good, whether somebody is laughing, whether there is work to do and whether the people you love are nearby.

Looking back, I can see how much resourcefulness was built into those years. If something was needed, you figured out what you had and tried to make it work. Money could solve problems, but when money was scarce, ingenuity had to do some of the work instead.

That is one of the ideas at the heart of Three-Quarters Green: humble circumstances do not make a life insignificant. Sometimes the details people are tempted to hide become the very details worth preserving.

The things memory keeps

Years later, I do not remember childhood as a balance sheet. I remember objects, voices, smells and moments. I remember the kind of details that seemed too ordinary to matter when they were happening.

That is one reason I wrote Three-Quarters Green. Fiction gave me a way to take the texture of a working-class childhood—the old cars, trailers, jobs, family sayings, mistakes, laughter and hard seasons—and build a story around the larger truth hiding inside them.

The larger truth is simple: a life does not have to look impressive from the outside to be worth remembering.

Why tell these stories now?

Because time changes the value of ordinary things. What once felt inconvenient can become a memory you would gladly step inside for five more minutes. The house gets replaced. People grow older. Some of them leave us. Eventually you realize that the small things were never really small.

That realization runs through much of my writing. I write about working people and family because those are the people I know. I write about memory because the older I get, the more I understand how much of a life lives there.
